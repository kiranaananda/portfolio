Google Travel Revamp: Bridging Trip Planning and Booking

Planning tools don’t book, and booking tools don’t plan. This one-month concept project asks how Google Travel could reduce cognitive load and fragmentation for people planning a trip, moving from competitive analysis and interviews to journey maps, an ideation workshop, wireframes and a usability test plan.

Company Context

Google Travel launched in 2019 by consolidating Google Flights, Google Hotels and Google Trips into a single platform. Google doesn’t report its revenue separately, but the business model for travel is referral fees from airlines, hotels and online travel agencies, on top of its advertising business. The audience is B2C: anyone planning leisure or business travel. The goal I worked from is to become the primary source for travel planning and booking by capturing users at the inspiration phase.

The UX Challenge: A Gap Between Two Mindsets

Users struggle to bridge planning and booking, which raises cognitive load and creates fragmentation. Planning tools like spreadsheets, Notion and email are slow and analytical, while booking sites are fast, urgent and emotional.

The Planning Phase

Users organize logic, budgets and desires. This is slow, analytical, calculating, System 2 thinking.

Tools: Google Sheets, Gmail, Notion, Tripadvisor, TripIt

The Booking Phase

Users execute transactions and lock in inventory. It is fast and urgent, System 1 (“Only 1 room left!”), with a System 2 option of “let’s book it for later.”

Tools: Google Flights, Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia, Kayak

Insight
The Failure: The User Is Left Alone in the Middle
  • Planning tools don’t book. Booking tools don’t plan. The user has to manage the chaos between them.
  • Some users do a System 2 task, complex planning, using System 1 tools, the sales-focused booking sites.

1. Competitive Landscape: The “Live Planning” Quadrant Is Empty

I compared Google Travel with direct competitors (TripIt, Wanderlog) and indirect ones (Notion, Airtable, Excel). Planning tools hold static data and booking tools hold live data, but none do both. The opportunity is a tool where prices update automatically before you decide.

2. Foundational Research: In-depth Interviews
Research Plan and Scope
Objective
Understand the cognitive and emotional friction points in order to design a seamless planning-to-booking workflow.
Research questions
What emotional triggers make a traveler track a flight rather than book it? How do travelers react when their mental model of a trip conflicts with separate bookings? What creates a sense of confidence or control while planning and booking?
Planned design
Target of 16 participants, a 12-question screener, behavioral segments with hard, minimum and soft quotas, and a four-part discussion guide (about 45 minutes).
What I completed
Within the one-month timeline I completed 2 interviews, one for each persona. The themes below are exploratory and need more interviews to validate.
Three Key Areas Where Current Tools Fail to Match User Intent
The Discovery Gap

Users will change destinations for value, but they have to hunt for alternatives by hand because search tools are rigid.

“It’s about the theme, not the place. If I like to have beaches, I’ll find alternatives that offer the same vibes.”

ImpactSearch needs a thematic entry point, not only a fixed destination.

The “Anchor” vs. the “Whole”

Users plan non-linearly, but tools force a linear checkout process.

“I don’t book all accommodations in advance because what if I like the place and I want to stay one more day here?”

ImpactPeople should be able to book one anchor, such as a flight, and fill in the rest later.

Total Cost Blind Spot

The lack of cost simulation is what drives spreadsheet usage.

“With spreadsheet I can calculate the estimated cost and make scenario about order of places and its price.”

ImpactShowing total trip cost side by side could remove the need to leave for a spreadsheet.

3. Conceptual Components: Journey Maps

Each interview became a persona and a journey map. Switch between them to compare how a planner and a spontaneous explorer move through the same trip.

What the Two Journeys Suggest

Saved & Discover

A tab that handles locations and broad categories, for people who start with a theme rather than a destination.

Trip Dashboard

A dashboard that is strict but also flexible and editable on the fly. An Airbnb-style itinerary with hierarchical views, where users can create, edit and share personalized schedules.

Trips Don’t End at Booking

The system must understand that a trip isn’t finished just because a flight is booked.

4. Ideation Workshop

I structured a 90-minute workshop around a warm-up, problem mapping to find breakpoints in the journey, Crazy 8’s sketching, strategy prioritization on a value-versus-effort matrix, and detailed wireframe sketching. The breakpoints it surfaced: no shared inspiration board or group polling, limited thematic search, no collaborative workspace, no integrated booking for multi-part trips, no consolidated trip dashboard, no clear cost comparison across destinations, and no support for anchor bookings.

The Quick Wins

Anchor booking logic: let users book a flight without nagging them to book a hotel, changing the trip’s state from “Incomplete” to “Open-ended.”

The Active Trip: auto-group searched items into a Trip folder that can grow into a dashboard and later an itinerary.

ValueHigh user and business value, low effort.

The Big Bets

Complex Scenario Planner: a tool that simulates “Option A vs Option B” with live pricing or with AI.

ValueHigh user and business value, high effort.

Fills-in

The Trip Digest notification: batch price-change alerts instead of emailing every time a tracked flight moves.

ValueLower value, low effort.

The Money Pit

Tonight Mode: location-aware logic and real-time hotel availability for a last-minute “panic” moment. HotelTonight and Kayak already offer similar functions.

ValueLower value, high effort, deprioritized.

6. Usability Testing Plan

This step is a plan and has not been run. It would evaluate whether the Active Trip and Scenario Planner let users plan complex trips without resorting to spreadsheets or notes, using moderated remote sessions with a think-aloud protocol and five participants (three planners, two spontaneous explorers).

Task 1: The Anchor Booking

Spontaneous explorer. Book a flight to Miami while keeping the trip active so a hotel can be added later.

SuccessBooks the flight without an error state or the urge to start a new search.

Task 2: Building Scenarios

Planner. From 3 saved flights and 3 hotels for a Tokyo trip, create a Luxury and a Budget option.

SuccessUses the selection and group functions without asking for help.

Task 3: The Comparison

Planner. Compare the total cost of both scenarios side by side and find the saving.

SuccessFinds the difference in the UI without a calculator or notes.

Why It Could Matter for Google

These are hypotheses to test, not results. Keeping people in a single Trip from inspiration through booking could capture users who currently leave for spreadsheets and booking sites, which fits the goal of owning the inspiration phase. Anchor booking could turn a partial booking into a continuing relationship rather than a lost one. And a scenario view that shows total cost could raise confidence at the moment of choosing, which is where referral fees are earned.

A note on the data

This was a one-month academic project. Findings come from two interviews, so they are directional rather than representative, and I would validate them with the full planned sample before drawing conclusions. The usability test has not been run. Competitive observations reflect the products when I did the analysis and may have changed. Next steps are the full interview round, then running the test plan above and revising the wireframes from what it shows.

Retrospective

This project taught me to follow a design from a tension, planning versus booking, through to a specific screen, and to tag each design decision with the research theme behind it. With more time I would complete the interviews, run the usability test, and check which ideas are feasible with the people who would build them.
